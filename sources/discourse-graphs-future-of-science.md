## Overview

This Protocol Labs interview has Tom Kalil question Evan Miyazono and Matt Akamatsu about discourse graphs for scientific collaboration. It introduces the question-claim-evidence schema, reports use in the Akamatsu Lab, and discusses advantages, adoption barriers, and possible pilots for researchers, funders, publishers, and tool builders. It is useful primary testimony from practitioners and clearly distinguishes experience from aspirations, but many benefits remain proposals or anecdotes rather than controlled findings. For research-wiki organization and sharing, it adds an important boundary to the federation sources: a team's live graph can support close collaborators, while sharing beyond that group requires selecting and contextualizing material.

## Key points

- A discourse graph labels brief notes as questions, claims, or evidence and connects them according to their relationships. ("What is a discourse graph, and how are scientists beginning to use them?")
- Akamatsu Lab members share one graph containing article evidence, working hypotheses, experiment requests, and original results. ("The Akamatsu Lab shares a discourse graph")
- Within teams, graphs have enough implicit context for close collaborators, but wider publication requires preparing a subset of pages with additional context. (footnote 1)
- A working graph does not carry universal context; much of it may initially be usable only by close collaborators. ("What do you view as the primary obstacles")
- Discourse graphs are most useful as extensions of ongoing individual or group notetaking and sensemaking, not as a separate formalization layer. ("Lastly, DGs do not carry universal context")
- One intermediate sharing practice is to keep results within a trusted network such as a lab until they are ready for wider release. (footnote 4)
